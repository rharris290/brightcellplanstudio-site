Beginner budget spreadsheet guide

How to choose a first budget method, set up categories, and keep a simple spreadsheet up to date, including the 50/30/20 split.

A budget spreadsheet is a place to write down the money you expect to receive and the money you plan to spend, then compare that plan with what actually happened. You do not need to be "good with numbers." You need a method you will look at more than once. This guide is about choosing that method and setting up your first month. When you are ready to open a file in Google Sheets, use our separate walkthrough, How to use a budget spreadsheet in Google Sheets.

1. Gather last month's real numbers

Before you pick a template, spend a short time with last month's bank and card activity, plus any cash you spent. Write down:

  • Income you actually received, after taxes, from each source (paychecks, side work, benefits and so on)
  • Fixed bills, such as rent or housing, utilities, insurance, subscriptions and minimum loan payments
  • Variable spending, such as groceries, fuel, eating out and household bits

Using after-tax income keeps the plan closer to what arrives in your account. One real month is enough to start. You can refine the categories later.

2. Choose a simple method

You can change methods later. These three are a solid starting point:

  • 50/30/20. Split after-tax income into needs (about 50%), wants (about 30%), and savings or debt payoff (about 20%). It is a starting point, not a rule. If housing alone takes more than half your income, adjust the split until it fits your life.
  • Paycheck budgeting. Plan each paycheck on its own: which bills that check needs to cover before the next one arrives. This suits people who are paid weekly or biweekly and run short between checks.
  • A simple monthly plan. List income at the top, assign a planned amount to each category, and leave a line for savings or extra debt payments. Some people like to give every dollar a job (sometimes called a zero-based budget). Others are happy with a few broad categories and a leftover line. Either is fine when you are starting.

If you like to set cash aside in envelopes, a cash-envelope tracker can sit next to any of these methods. It is a way of holding the money, not a different math.

3. Set up your categories

Start with a short list you will actually fill in, then add more only if you need them. A beginner sheet often has:

  • Needs: housing, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, minimum debt payments
  • Wants: dining out, streaming or hobby spending, gifts you choose to buy
  • Savings and debt payoff: emergency savings, sinking funds for irregular bills, and any extra you can put toward debt

Type only into the input cells. Cells with formulas calculate totals for you, so leave those alone. If a template has a dashboard, that is usually where the planned and actual totals show up once you have entered the month.

4. Enter the plan first, then the actuals

At the start of the month, or when a paycheck arrives, fill in planned amounts. During the month, add what you actually spent. Comparing the two is the point of the sheet. A category that runs ahead of the plan is information, not a failure. You can move money from a category that still has room, or trim that category next month.

5. Review once a week, and keep going

Set a short weekly reminder to enter new transactions and look at the totals. Weekly is often easier than trying to remember everything at the end of the month. After two or three months you will see which categories were too tight or too loose, and you can adjust the plan instead of starting over.

A bill calendar is a useful companion if due dates, not just totals, are what catch you out. If paying down balances is your main goal, continue with How to pay off debt with a spreadsheet.
