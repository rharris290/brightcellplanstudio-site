How to pay off debt with a spreadsheet

List what you owe, choose a payoff method such as snowball or avalanche, and track every payment in a spreadsheet.

A debt payoff spreadsheet will not pay the bills for you, but it will put every balance, interest rate and payment in one place so you can see a plan and stick to it. This guide walks through a simple setup you can use in Excel or Google Sheets. If you are also building a first monthly budget, start with our beginner budget spreadsheet guide, then come back to this one for the debts themselves.

1. List every debt in one place

Open a tracker, or a blank sheet, and make one row for each debt. For each one, write down:

  • The name, such as a card, a car loan, a student loan or a medical bill
  • The current balance
  • The interest rate, if you have it. The statement or lender website usually shows this.
  • The minimum payment
  • The due date

Use today's balances, not last month's. A complete list matters more than a perfect design. If you are not sure of a rate, leave it blank for now and keep the debt on the list.

2. Understand snowball and avalanche

Two common ways to decide which debt to pay extra toward are the snowball method and the avalanche method. Both assume you keep making at least the minimum payment on every debt.

  • Snowball: put any extra money toward the debt with the smallest balance first, then roll that payment into the next-smallest when the first one is gone. The idea is to clear accounts sooner so the plan feels like it is moving.
  • Avalanche: put any extra money toward the debt with the highest interest rate first, then move to the next-highest rate. The idea is to reduce the amount of interest that adds up over time.

Neither method is a rule. Some people prefer snowball because a small balance can be cleared sooner. Some prefer avalanche because it targets the costliest interest first. Some use a custom order, such as a debt they want gone for a deadline. A good tracker lets you compare these plans with your own numbers rather than asking you to guess.

3. Keep the minimums going, and add extra to one target

A simple monthly rhythm looks like this:

  • Pay at least the minimum on every debt, on time, so you avoid late fees and extra interest where you can.
  • Choose one target debt, using snowball, avalanche or your own order.
  • Send any leftover money that month to that one target. When it is paid off, add what you had been paying on it to the next target.

If money is tight some months, paying the minimums and holding the plan is still progress. You can raise the extra payment again when there is room.

4. Log payments and look at the plan, not just the balance

Once a month, or whenever you make a payment, add it to a payment log: the date, which debt, and the amount. Then look at the plan view, not only the current balance. A useful tracker will show, from the numbers you entered, an estimated payoff date and how much interest each method would accrue. Those figures come from your own balances, rates and payments. They will change if you add a payment, miss one, or the rate changes, so treat them as a working estimate.

5. Plan ahead for irregular expenses

A paid-off debt can be replaced by a new one if a car repair, holiday or insurance bill arrives with no money set aside. A sinking-funds tracker is a way to save a little each month toward those known, irregular costs so they do not have to go on a card. It sits beside a debt plan rather than replacing it.

A mortgage or other long loan can also be modelled on its own if you want to see how an extra payment would change the payoff date. That is a supplement to the main list, not a second system.

6. Keep a simple budget running alongside

The extra money for debt payoff has to come from somewhere. A straightforward monthly or paycheck budget shows what is left after needs are covered, and where you can free a little more.
